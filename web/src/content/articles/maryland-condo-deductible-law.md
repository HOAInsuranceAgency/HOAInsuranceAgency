> Maryland's Chapter 717 of 2026 (Senate Bill 747) changes how condominium property losses are shared. When damage starts inside a unit, the most that unit's owner can owe toward the association's master policy deductible rises from $10,000 to $25,000. The law also requires most residential condo owners to carry their own unit policy with set minimums. Both changes take effect October 1, 2027. A 12-month master policy written this winter will still be in force on that date, so boards should settle their deductible, owner notices and proof-of-Insurance process during this renewal and budget season.

## What Maryland's new condo deductible law changes

Senate Bill 747 was approved by the Governor on May 26, 2026, and became [Chapter 717 of the 2026 Laws of Maryland](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). The [Maryland General Assembly's bill record](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/sb0747?ys=2026RS) lists an effective date of October 1, 2027, and a cross-filed House bill, HB 469. Rather than starting on October 1, 2026, as is typical, the law was given a delayed date to allow insurers and owners time to provide and obtain the required coverage, according to [Lerch, Early & Brewer](https://www.lerchearly.com/news/2026-community-associations-maryland-legislative-updates/).

Some background helps. Under Section 11-114 of the Maryland Condominium Act, the council of unit owners (the statute's term for the condominium association) must maintain property Insurance, to the extent reasonably available, on the common elements and, except in condominiums made up entirely of similar detached units, on the units too. That coverage excludes improvements and betterments installed by unit owners other than the developer, [as the statute states](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf), meaning upgrades owners added after the developer's original construction. This association policy is often called the master policy. Its deductible is the amount the association pays out of pocket on a covered loss before the insurer pays the rest, as the [Cowie Law Group explains](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/).

The law makes two changes. First, it raises the share of the master deductible owed by an owner whose unit is where the damage started. Second, it requires most residential condominium owners to keep their own unit policy with minimum coverages and to show proof every year. Both are summarized in the [Department of Legislative Services fiscal and policy note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0007/sb0747.pdf).

## Who pays the master deductible now and after October 2027

Under Maryland law, the deductible follows the source of the damage. If damage starts in the common elements, or comes from an event outside the units and common elements, the association's deductible is a common expense, meaning a cost shared by all owners through the association, [under Section 11-114](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). [Cowie's examples](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/) are a common roof leak that damages units below and a storm that blows siding off the building. If the damage starts inside a unit, current law makes that unit's owner responsible for the deductible up to $10,000, as the [fiscal note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0007/sb0747.pdf) explains. Cowie's example of unit-originating damage is a leaking pipe in one unit that damages a common hallway ceiling or the unit below.

From October 1, 2027, the owner of the unit where the damage originated will be responsible for the least of three amounts: the total cost of the repair or replacement, the association's property Insurance deductible as specified in its policy, or $25,000. Any part of the deductible above the owner's share stays a common expense, under the amended text in [Chapter 717](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). The law also removes the limit that let the association assess the responsible owner only annually, according to the [fiscal note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0007/sb0747.pdf).

A hypothetical shows the effect. Picture a building with a $50,000 master deductible and a $60,000 covered water loss that starts in one unit. Today that owner's share is capped at $10,000 and the other $40,000 is a common expense; after October 1, 2027, the owner's share rises to $25,000 and the common expense falls to $25,000. If the same building carried a $10,000 deductible, the owner's share would be $10,000 under both versions. Under the new formula, a repair that cost less than both the deductible and $25,000 would leave the owner responsible for the repair cost.

The association must still tell each owner in writing every year what they are responsible for and the amount of the deductible as specified in the master policy, [under the amended law](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). The [fiscal note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0007/sb0747.pdf) explains that the law changes this notice requirement to reflect the owner's new responsibility, so the notice will need new wording once the $25,000 formula applies.

## Why the deductible belongs on this renewal agenda

The law does not set the master deductible. The formula uses the deductible as specified in the association's own property Insurance policy, [under Chapter 717](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf), so what changes is who absorbs it. Once the law takes effect, any part of the deductible above the owner's share is paid as a common expense. In a building with a deductible above $25,000, a large loss caused by one unit will still spread part of the deductible across all owners. Damage that originates from the common elements or from an outside event, such as a storm, remains a common expense whatever the deductible, [under Section 11-114](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf).

Timing matters because a 12-month master policy that starts after October 1, 2026 will still be in force on October 1, 2027. The deductible chosen at this season's renewal may therefore be the one in place when the new formula begins. [Whiteford's 2026 client alert](https://www.whitefordlaw.com/news-events/client-alert-2026-maryland-legislative-update-for-common-ownership-communities) advises boards to review the deductible with their Insurance broker and consider whether adjustments are needed.

When comparing quotes, ask your broker to show each deductible option next to two figures: how much of a unit-caused loss the responsible owner would carry under the new formula, and how much would fall to the association. That framing helps the board explain its choice to owners in the annual notice. ## The new unit owner Insurance requirement

New Section 11-114.3 requires an owner in a condominium made up entirely of residential units to keep a condominium unit owner policy, or a substantially similar property policy, on the unit, [according to Chapter 717](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). The unit owner policy is commonly called an HO-6, [as Cowie notes](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/). The requirement does not apply to detached units in a condominium made up entirely of similar detached units.

The required policy must include three things, as listed in the [fiscal note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0007/sb0747.pdf). It must cover the association's property Insurance deductible, or the owner's portion of it. It must include at least $25,000 of loss assessment coverage, which [Cowie describes](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/) as helping pay an owner's share of a special assessment when the master policy does not fully cover a loss, for example because of a large deductible. It must also carry dwelling or building coverage of at least the greater of $25,000 or the amount needed to repair or replace the owner's improvements and betterments and any upgrades the bylaws make the owner responsible for insuring.

Earlier versions of the bill went further. Before amendment, they would also have required contents coverage, 12 months of loss-of-use coverage for alternate housing and at least $500,000 of personal liability coverage, but those were struck before passage, [Cowie reports](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/). Language that would have let the association buy a policy for an owner who did not comply, and charge the premium back as an assessment, was also struck from the [enacted text](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf). Owners remain free to buy additional Insurance for their own benefit, [the law confirms](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf).

Two other provisions matter to boards. Condominium bylaws may not require an owner to carry coverage amounts beyond the statutory minimums, and owners must give the association evidence of the required policy every year and whenever the association asks. If an owner does not carry the policy, that does not limit the owner's responsibility for unit-originated damage, [under new Section 11-114.3](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_717_sb0747E.pdf).

## How Maryland boards and managers can prepare

Start with your documents. If your bylaws or rules require owners to carry higher limits than the new minimums, those requirements may conflict with the cap on bylaw requirements. [Cowie recommends](https://cowielawgroup.com/cowie-law-group-maryland-hoa-condo-laws-2026-2027-legislative-update/) reviewing bylaws and rules for consistency, coordinating with Insurance professionals and legal counsel on enforcement, and updating resale, owner handbook and management materials. Because the enacted law dropped the proposed power to buy coverage for owners who do not comply, ask counsel what enforcement tools your own documents provide.

Next, plan the paperwork. [Whiteford](https://www.whitefordlaw.com/news-events/client-alert-2026-maryland-legislative-update-for-common-ownership-communities) suggests educating owners well before October 2027, building a system to collect and track proof-of-Insurance certificates each year, and drafting an annual notice that states the deductible and the owner's financial responsibility. Managers who already collect HO-6 certificates for some owners can extend that process to every unit and set a date each year when evidence is due.

Finally, connect the law to day-to-day risk. Because owners can carry a larger share of losses that start in their units, the annual notice is a good place to remind them of this. Damage that starts in a unit, such as a leaking pipe, can make that owner responsible for up to $25,000 under the new formula. Clear, early communication now may help prevent disputes after the first loss under the new rules.
