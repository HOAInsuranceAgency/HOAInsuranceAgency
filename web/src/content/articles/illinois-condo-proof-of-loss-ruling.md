> An Illinois condominium association has lost a $4.3 million storm claim because the proof of loss it filed was not properly sworn. On November 19, 2026, a federal appeals court ruled that a notary page copied from an earlier, lower-valued draft did not count, so the policy's lawsuit deadline was never paused and the association's suit came too late. Here is what happened, what the court said, and the claim habits that help Illinois boards avoid the same result.

## What happened at Tower Crossing in Glenview, Illinois

Tower Crossing Condominium Association manages a condominium complex in Glenview, Illinois, and was insured by Affiliated FM Insurance Company under an all-risk policy, according to the [Seventh Circuit's opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0). An all-risk policy, also called open peril, covers risks not explicitly excluded in the policy, as the [NAIC's glossary](https://content.naic.org/glossary-insurance-terms) explains. The association says a storm damaged the complex's roof on July 2, 2019. It gave the insurer notice of the damage on August 21, 2019, which began two years of discussion, investigation and negotiation, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) recounts.

Two clauses in the policy decided the case. The first required a "signed and sworn" proof of loss within 90 days after the loss unless the insurer extended that time in writing, as the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) quotes. A proof of loss is a formal statement the insured makes to the insurance company about a loss, giving the company enough information to decide its liability under the policy, according to the [Washington State Office of the Insurance Commissioner's glossary](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/consumers-insurance-glossary). This one had to state five categories of information, including the actual cash value and replacement value of each item. The second clause required any lawsuit to be filed within two years of the loss, extended by the days between filing a proof of loss and the insurer's denial of the claim in whole or in part.

As the two years ran down, the insurer agreed to push the lawsuit deadline to October 2, 2021, and the two sides kept investigating and negotiating, the [court said](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0). On September 30, 2021, the board president signed a proof of loss valuing roof replacement at about $2.95 million and had it notarized, but did not submit it. The next day the association submitted a different proof of loss valued at about $4.34 million, carrying the same notary certificate, dated September 30 with an identically placed stamp, as [Insurance Business reported](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/risk-compliance-legal/copypasted-notary-page-sinks-condo-associations-4-3m-storm-claim-598269.aspx).

The insurer rejected that proof of loss on October 29, 2021, without making a final coverage decision, and the association sued on November 19, 2021, for breach of contract and bad faith, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) states. The insurer's own estimate of the damage was about $204,000, according to the [district court's ruling](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/illinois/ilndce/1:2021cv06228/409108/146/) and [Business Insurance](https://www.businessinsurance.com/condo-board-that-reused-notarization-loses-storm-damage-suit/). The insurer spotted the reused notary page during discovery, the process by which lawyers learn about the other side's case before trial, as the [federal courts' glossary](https://www.uscourts.gov/glossary) defines it. In a declaration, the board president admitted that the association had "attached" his "sworn signature" from the unsubmitted proof of loss to the submitted one, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) recounts.

## What the court said "sworn" means

The district court granted summary judgment for the insurer, and a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit affirmed on November 19, 2026, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) shows. Summary judgment is a decision made on the statements and evidence in the record without a trial, according to the [federal courts' glossary](https://www.uscourts.gov/glossary). Because the case was in federal court on diversity jurisdiction, the panel applied Illinois law. It held that the word "sworn" unambiguously requires an oath, and said "sworn" may not require notarization specifically, but notarization is one way to take that oath.

The association argued that "sworn" only meant a promise, relying on shortened dictionary quotations. The panel read the full definitions in Black's Law Dictionary, Merriam-Webster, Cambridge and Dictionary.com and found that each pointed to an oath, as [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/risk-compliance-legal/copypasted-notary-page-sinks-condo-associations-4-3m-storm-claim-598269.aspx) summarized. It also reasoned that if "sworn" meant no more than a promise, it would add little or nothing to "signed," and Illinois courts read Insurance policies to give effect to each provision, according to the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0).

The court then explained why the copied page failed. Under the Illinois notary statutes it cited, an oath attaches only to the particular statement presented to a notary on a single occasion. The board president swore to the $2.95 million version, and approving the reuse of that page on a document worth about $1.4 million more was not an oath to the new statement, the [court held](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0).

The association also argued that the oath served no purpose because the insurer had already investigated the claim. The panel disagreed, saying the oath's objective is to prevent fraud by binding the policyholder to a specific statement, as [Business Insurance reported](https://www.businessinsurance.com/condo-board-that-reused-notarization-loses-storm-damage-suit/). Whether the insurer actually needed or used the information, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) added, did not change what the policy required.

## Why the missed oath meant a missed deadline

The case turned on an Illinois statute, 215 ILCS 5/143.1. As quoted in the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0), it pauses, or "tolls," an Insurance policy's lawsuit deadline from the date a proof of loss is filed "in whatever form is required by the policy" until the claim is denied in whole or in part. Because this policy required a signed and sworn proof of loss, an unsworn one did not start the pause.

That meant the extended deadline of October 2, 2021 simply ran out. The association's lawsuit, filed on November 19, 2021, was time-barred, according to the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0). As a result, the $4.3 million claim never made it to trial, and [Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/risk-compliance-legal/copypasted-notary-page-sinks-condo-associations-4-3m-storm-claim-598269.aspx) drew the lesson that proof of loss form requirements are strictly enforced.

The association's fallback was "substantial compliance," the idea that a filing that is close enough should count. The court rejected it for tolling purposes, citing earlier decisions applying Illinois law that hold that the insured must do more than present information equivalent to what the policy asks for, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) explains. The panel noted that whether a proof of loss is good enough to satisfy a coverage condition is a separate question from whether it starts tolling, and the statute's text controls the second one.

The court also noted that the association's notice of damage in August 2019 was not a proof of loss, as the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) states. For boards, that distinction matters: reporting a claim and filing the sworn statement the policy calls for are two different steps, each with its own timing in the policy.

## What Illinois boards should take from the ruling

The first lesson is to know exactly what your own master policy requires. Find the proof of loss requirement and the clause that sets a deadline for suing the insurer, and note any required form, oath and time limit. The Tower Crossing policy required a sworn proof of loss within 90 days after the loss, unless extended in writing, and a lawsuit within two years, as the [court described](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0), and those terms, read with the Illinois statute, controlled the outcome. Your policy may say something different, which is why it pays to read it before a loss rather than after.

The second lesson is that every version needs its own oath. If the claim figure changes, for example after a contractor or adjuster revises an estimate, the new proof of loss should be signed and sworn again before it goes to the insurer. In this case the [court said](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) the oath covered only the statement the board president attested to on September 30, not the later document with a higher figure.

The third lesson is about timing. The association submitted its proof of loss one day before the extended lawsuit deadline was set to expire, according to the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0), which left no room to fix a defect. Boards and managers can put the date of loss, the proof of loss due date and the lawsuit deadline on the calendar as soon as a claim is reported, track any written extensions, and ask association counsel well before a deadline whether a lawsuit should be filed to protect the claim.

Finally, be careful with shortcuts on sworn documents. The association pointed to statutes that give certain written declarations the same effect as if sworn under oath, but the court did not decide whether such a declaration would have satisfied this policy, because nothing of that sort happened here, the [opinion](https://media.ca7.uscourts.gov/cgi-bin/OpinionsWeb/processWebInputExternal.pl?Submit=Display&Path=Y2026/D11-19/C:25-3167:J:Brennan:aut:T:fnOp:N:3645247:S:0) notes. Unless the association's attorney advises otherwise, the safer course is a fresh oath before a notary for each proof of loss you file.

## A claims routine that holds up for Illinois associations

Good claim handling starts with clear roles. Decide in advance who on the board or management team reports a loss, who gathers photos and estimates, who signs the proof of loss on behalf of the association, and who keeps the master file of everything sent to and received from the insurer. Record board decisions about the claim in the minutes so the file shows who approved each submission.

Keep the paper trail tight. Each proof of loss should be a complete document that is signed and sworn as submitted, with a copy of exactly what went to the insurer kept in the claim file. Ask for any extension of time in writing, and confirm extensions you receive by return email or letter so there is no dispute about the new date.

If outside professionals such as public adjusters, engineers, contractors or attorneys work on a claim, make sure each of them knows the policy's deadlines and form requirements, and that no one assembles a filing by reusing signature or notary pages from another document. The Tower Crossing ruling applies Illinois law and one specific policy, so associations in other states should check their own policy wording and state law rather than assume the same result.
