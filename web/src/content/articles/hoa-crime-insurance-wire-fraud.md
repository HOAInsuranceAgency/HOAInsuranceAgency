> Complaints to the FBI reported more than $3 billion in business email compromise losses in 2025. The scheme uses hijacked email or phone accounts and social engineering to redirect payments to criminals. For HOA and condominium boards, that can mean a manager or treasurer wiring association funds to a fake vendor account. This guide explains how fidelity, crime and social engineering coverage differ, what Fannie Mae requires, and the controls and renewal questions that help keep a payment scam from becoming an uninsured loss.

## Why wire fraud belongs on the board agenda

The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) received 24,768 business email compromise complaints in 2025, with reported losses of $3,046,598,558, up from $2,770,151,146 in 2024, according to its [2025 Internet Crime Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf). Across all crime types, reported losses reached $20.877 billion, a 26% increase from 2024.

Business email compromise, often shortened to BEC, is defined in the [IC3 report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) as a scam aimed at businesses or individuals who work with suppliers or regularly make wire transfer payments. The criminals compromise email accounts, phone numbers or virtual meeting tools through social engineering or computer intrusion, then use that access to move funds without authorization. The FBI report does not single out community associations, so it cannot tell boards how many victims were HOAs or condominiums.

Attorneys who represent associations have described how the scam reaches them. In a [case summarized by Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fraud-prevention), a property management company received emailed wire instructions for a vendor invoice on behalf of a condominium. The email changed the prior payment method but appeared to come from a known contact, and the manager wired about $50,000 to an account the criminal controlled. The vendor was never paid, and the condominium was out the money.

Associations also depend on a large number of volunteers. The [Foundation for Community Association Research's 2025 Statistical Review](https://foundation.caionline.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2025StatisticalReviewFoundation.pdf) counts 2,555,000 elected board members and appointed committee members in U.S. community associations. Social engineering schemes target board members, managers and others with access to association funds, as the next section explains.

## Fidelity, crime and social engineering are not the same thing

A [guide to fidelity coverage](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fidelity-coverage-essential) from Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks, a condominium and community association law firm, separates two kinds of loss. It says associations need fidelity coverage for fraud by an employee, board member or volunteer. They also need crime coverage for crimes by outsiders, such as a bank employee who misdirects wire transfers or a hacker who siphons funds from association accounts. The guide adds that many fidelity policies cover both kinds of loss but some do not, and an association whose policy leaves out crime should buy that coverage separately.

Social engineering is a third idea. [Kevin Davis Insurance Services](https://www.kdisonline.com/defending-against-deception-safeguarding-hoa-funds-from-social-engineering-fraud/) describes it as a scam in which fraudsters manipulate people into revealing confidential information or authorizing fraudulent transactions. It says that in HOAs, these scams target board members, managers and others with access to community funds. Its examples include a scammer posing as a maintenance provider who requests urgent payment, or posing as a board member who requests a funds transfer. In these cases, the person who sends the money is an authorized insider who has been fooled, not a thief.

Crime Insurance programs for associations may give these losses separate coverage parts with different limits. One program from [Community Association Insurance Solutions](https://www.caislive.com/post/get-best-in-class-community-association-crime-insurance-with-social-engineering) lists employee theft limits from $25,000 up to $6 million in all states except Kentucky, New York and Virginia, where the top limit is $1 million. The same program includes a computer and funds transfer fraud limit equal to the employee theft limit, and a separate $25,000 limit for fraudulent impersonation, which it also calls social engineering. That is one program, not a market standard, but it shows why a board should not assume the largest limit on the policy applies to every type of crime loss.

## What Fannie Mae requires for fidelity and crime coverage

[Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fidelity-coverage-essential) notes that both state laws and secondary market rules require fidelity Insurance for many community associations. [Fannie Mae's Selling Guide](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b7-4-02/fidelitycrime-insurance-requirements-project-developments) requires fidelity/crime Insurance for all condominium and co-op projects, with three exceptions: projects that qualify for a waiver of project review, projects of 20 units or fewer, and projects whose required coverage would be $5,000 or less. Where the rule applies, the policy must cover the dishonest or fraudulent acts of anyone who handles or is responsible for association funds, whether or not that person is paid. It must also cover the acts of any management agent.

The required amount depends on the association's financial controls. Under the [same Selling Guide section](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b7-4-02/fidelitycrime-insurance-requirements-project-developments), coverage must equal at least three months of assessments on all units if the association follows at least one of three controls. The first is separate operating and reserve bank accounts, each with access controls, with monthly statements sent directly to the association. The second is a management company that keeps separate records and accounts for each association and cannot draw checks on or transfer funds from the reserve account. The third is a rule that two board members sign any check written on the reserve account. Without at least one of those controls, coverage must equal the maximum funds the association or its management agent holds at any time.

Fannie Mae also requires the association to be the named insured, with premiums paid as a common expense. A policy held by the management company in its own name is not an acceptable substitute, although Fannie Mae says the management agent should carry its own coverage in addition. Where a state has its own statutory fidelity or crime Insurance requirement, Fannie Mae accepts that requirement in place of its own. Notice that this requirement is written around the people who handle the money and does not mention impersonation or funds transfer fraud, so meeting it does not answer the wire fraud question on its own. Our [condo Insurance](/condo-insurance/) page covers association coverage more broadly.

Lender and statutory minimums are a floor. The [Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks guide](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fidelity-coverage-essential) notes that association balances change month to month, and that an Insurance payout, for example, can temporarily swell the operating account by a large amount. It calls basing coverage on the maximum amount potentially at risk the best practice. It also cites a rule of thumb from Insurance experts: buy coverage equal to the combined peak totals of assessments and reserves, or as close to that as the association can afford.

## Controls that prevent the loss and protect the claim

The attorneys who described the $50,000 case call this kind of fraud highly preventable. Their [recommended practices](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fraud-prevention) start with never relying on emailed wire instructions alone, especially when the instructions differ from earlier payments, arrive as a last-minute change or come with urgency. The most important step, they say, is a mandatory callback to a known contact at the vendor, using a phone number already on file rather than one supplied in the latest email. The call should be documented with the date, time and person reached. Their summary is blunt: "No verification = no wire."

The same article recommends treating any request to change bank accounts, switch from checks to wires, or pay close to a deadline as a high-risk event. It also calls for training staff and board members on common schemes such as business email compromise, spoofing and phishing, and on the association's approval and verification procedures. [Kevin Davis Insurance Services](https://www.kdisonline.com/defending-against-deception-safeguarding-hoa-funds-from-social-engineering-fraud/) adds clear protocols for verifying payment requests, regular training on recognizing phishing attempts and multifactor authentication for financial transactions.

These controls matter to the Insurance claim as well as to prevention. [Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fraud-prevention) warns that carriers frequently require procedures such as callback verification as a condition of coverage, and that if the procedures are not followed there could be no coverage for the claim. Its [fidelity guide](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fidelity-coverage-essential) makes the same point about financial safety measures some insurers require, so boards should confirm they are doing whatever their insurer requires.

A wire sent to a criminal may not even settle the bill. The firm explains that a payment obligation is generally discharged only when the intended party receives payment, so paying a fraudster does not typically satisfy the debt to the real vendor. It also notes that these losses can set off a dispute between the condominium and its management company over the disbursement of the funds.

## If money goes out the wrong door

The [FBI's guidance in the 2025 report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) says that when you discover a fraudulent transfer, "time is of the essence." Its advice is to immediately contact your financial institution and request a recall of the funds, along with any indemnification documents it needs. It also asks victims to file a complaint with IC3 regardless of the amount lost, including full transaction details, because IC3 may be able to help banks and law enforcement freeze funds.

That help can make a difference. The IC3 Recovery Asset Team works with financial institutions and FBI field offices to freeze fraudulent transfers. According to the [IC3 report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf), the team initiated 3,900 Financial Fraud Kill Chain incidents in 2025. Those incidents covered $1,163,919,846 in attempted theft, and $679,013,183 was frozen, a 58% success rate. The figures cover all fraud types handled through that process, not only business email compromise.

Notify your Insurance agent promptly as well. Review the policy's notice requirements so the claim is reported on time, and keep the emails, invoices and bank records that show how the payment happened.

## Questions to bring to your next renewal

Start with who the policy covers. The [Marcus, Errico, Emmer & Brooks guide](https://www.meeb.com/marcus-errico-emmer-brooks-newsletter/fidelity-coverage-essential) recommends that the policy identify directors, officers, volunteers and committee members by name as covered employees, or add them by endorsement, and that it name the manager as an insured. It also advises requiring the management company to carry its own fidelity coverage. That matches [Fannie Mae's](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b7-4-02/fidelitycrime-insurance-requirements-project-developments) expectation that a management agent handling association funds be covered by its own policy.

Then ask about each coverage part. Find out whether the policy includes crime coverage for outsiders as well as fidelity coverage for insiders. Ask which part would respond if a manager or treasurer were tricked into sending a payment, and what limit applies to that part. Ask what verification procedures the policy requires, and write those steps into the association's payment policy so every manager and officer follows them.

Finally, compare the limit to what is actually at risk. If the association is a condominium or co-op subject to Fannie Mae's rule, check that it meets the minimum. Then look at peak balances in the operating and reserve accounts, including months when large sums such as Insurance claim proceeds pass through the operating account.
