> When a covered loss hits property that every owner shares, or the master policy deductible is passed to owners, the bill can arrive as an assessment. This guide explains what loss assessment coverage on a condo unit-owners (HO-6) policy pays, why the standard amount is typically small, how one state sets a minimum, and what boards can tell owners now.

## What loss assessment coverage pays for

A loss assessment is a charge the association levies on owners after a loss to property they own together. [IRMI's Insurance glossary](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/loss-assessment) defines it as a property owner's share of a loss to property owned in common by all members of a property owners association. Loss assessment coverage is the part of a unit-owners policy that helps pay that share. The [Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/condo-insurance/learn-how-condo-insurance-works) notes that the policy an owner buys is sometimes referred to as an HO-6 policy, and that condo Insurance offers coverage similar to homeowners Insurance.

The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/insuring-a-co-op-or-condo), which calls it "unit assessment" coverage, describes it as reimbursing your share of an assessment charged to all unit owners because of a covered loss. Its example is a fire in the lobby, where every owner is charged for the repair. The same page explains that fully insuring a condo takes two policies: a master policy for the building, and an individual policy for the owner's liability, belongings and any structural elements of the unit the master policy does not cover.

Regulators describe the same situations. The [Colorado Division of Insurance](https://doi.colorado.gov/homeowners-hoainsurancetoolkit) says loss assessments occur when an association levies additional charges on owners to cover costs the master policy does not fully insure, such as deductibles or significant damages. Washington's regulator gives the example of wind damage to the roofs of several units that exceeds the master policy's coverage limits, leaving a special assessment to cover the rest of the damage.

## Why the master policy deductible can land on owners

A deductible, as Colorado's Division of Insurance defines it, is the out-of-pocket amount the policyholder pays before the Insurance pays for a claim. On a master policy, the policyholder is the association. Colorado tells owners in its [HOA Insurance toolkit](https://doi.colorado.gov/homeowners-hoainsurancetoolkit) that they may be responsible for the association's deductible and for any costs above the master policy's limits. Boards should confirm how their own governing documents allocate it.

Washington's regulator sorts master policies into three common types in its [condo Insurance guide](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/condo-insurance/learn-how-condo-insurance-works). An "all-in" policy covers the exterior and all interior finishes. A second type covers the unit only to its original interior and exterior finishes and leaves owner changes and upgrades out. A "bare walls" policy covers damage up to the uncovered sheetrock and subfloor. For each of the three types, the Washington guide says unit owners are responsible for the master policy or community deductible, which shows why the deductible matters even when the master policy is broad.

Master policy terms can also change. In the same [toolkit](https://doi.colorado.gov/homeowners-hoainsurancetoolkit), Colorado's regulator lists a tightening reinsurance market, and insurers being more insistent that properties be insured to value, among the factors behind rising HOA premiums. It adds that when an association adjusts its master policy's coverage, responsibility for certain parts of the property may shift to owners, and the association should inform owners so they can adjust their own coverage.

## What a standard HO-6 policy includes

Policies differ by carrier and edition, so each owner's own policy controls, and Washington's regulator advises checking with your agent or company to understand what your policy covers. A useful reference point is the standard unit-owners form HO 00 06 10 00, copyrighted by Insurance Services Office (ISO) in 1999. A [copy of that form posted by Nevada's Division of Insurance](https://docs.nv.gov/doi/documents/home_policies/HartfordForms/Hartford_HO_00_06_10_00.pdf) pays up to $1,000 for the owner's share of a loss assessment charged during the policy period by a corporation or association of property owners. The $1,000 is the most it pays for any one loss, regardless of the number of assessments.

That form also sets conditions. The assessment must result from a direct loss to property owned by all members collectively, of a type the policy would cover if the owner owned it, caused by a peril the policy insures against for the unit, other than earthquake or volcanic tremors. The form applies only one deductible per unit to any one loss and does not cover assessments charged by a governmental body. Washington's guide puts the practical test simply: if the cause of the damage is covered under your unit-owners policy, the loss assessment for that cause is also covered.

The default amount is modest. [IRMI](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/loss-assessment) notes that homeowners and unit-owners policies typically provide a small amount of loss assessment coverage, with more available by endorsement for an additional premium. An endorsement is an addition to a policy that changes or adds to its coverage or terms, as Colorado's toolkit defines it. If an owner's share of a master policy deductible could exceed the policy's loss assessment limit, the owner can ask an agent about adding more by endorsement.

### Liability assessments are a separate limit

The same ISO form contains a second, separate loss assessment coverage on the liability side of the policy. It pays up to $1,000 for an owner's share of an assessment that results from bodily injury or property damage the policy does not exclude. It also responds to assessments that arise from the acts of a director, officer or trustee acting in that role, provided the person is elected by the members and serves without deriving income from those duties. That second coverage also excludes assessments charged by a governmental body.

## Florida sets a minimum, and timing matters

States can set their own rules. In Florida, [section 627.714 of the Florida Statutes](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/627.714) requires unit-owner policies issued or renewed on or after July 1, 2010, to include at least $2,000 in property loss assessment coverage for all assessments arising from the same direct loss. The deductible on that coverage can be no more than $250 per direct property loss, and no deductible applies if one was or will be applied to the owner's other property loss from the same event. Owners and boards in Florida can find more on our [Florida HOA Insurance page](/hoa-insurance-florida/).

The Florida statute also addresses timing. The most a Florida unit owner's loss assessment coverage will pay for a loss equals the limit in effect one day before the occurrence that caused it, and changes made on or after that day do not apply to that loss. In other words, under Florida law, raising the limit on the day before a loss or afterward will not help with that loss. The statute also says each Florida unit owner's policy must state that its coverage is excess over the amount recoverable under any other policy covering the same property.

These timing rules come from Florida's statute. The ISO form described above ties its property loss assessment coverage to assessments charged during the policy period and states that its general policy-period condition does not apply to this coverage. Because wording varies, owners in every state should ask their agent how their own policy treats an assessment for a loss that happened before the current policy term began.

## Gaps loss assessment coverage will not fill

Loss assessment coverage follows the policy's covered causes of loss, so a cause the policy does not cover will not trigger it. Washington's guide says condo Insurance does not cover flood, earth movement, or wear and tear and maintenance items, and the ISO form expressly removes earthquake from its property loss assessment coverage. Under that form's wording, an assessment to fund routine repairs or reserves is not the result of a direct loss from an insured peril, so it does not meet the form's test.

The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/insuring-a-co-op-or-condo) says flood or earthquake Insurance may be necessary for owners in areas prone to those disasters. It also notes that sewer and drain backups are covered by neither the standard condo policy nor a flood policy, and it lists water backup coverage among the additional coverages owners may want to consider.

Owners should also remember that loss assessment coverage is only one piece of an HO-6 policy. The Insurance Information Institute explains that the individual policy covers personal possessions and any structural elements the master policy does not cover, along with liability and, likely, additional living expenses. An owner with a generous loss assessment limit can still be underinsured if the unit's interior finishes or upgrades are not properly insured.

## What boards and managers can do now

Owners can only choose the right limit if they know the deductible and the unit boundary. [Washington's regulator](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/condo-insurance/learn-how-condo-insurance-works) notes that each year when the community policy renews, owners receive a certificate of Insurance confirming the master policy's limits and deductible. Boards can go further by sending owners a plain summary of the deductible, how the governing documents allocate it, and where the master policy's coverage of the unit stops.

Colorado's Division of Insurance gives board members similar advice in its [toolkit](https://doi.colorado.gov/homeowners-hoainsurancetoolkit). It tells boards to review the master policy regularly, communicate changes in Insurance responsibilities to owners, understand how loss assessments and high deductibles affect the association and owners, and guide owners to align their own coverage with the association's. Among the questions it suggests owners ask their board is whether reserves are funded well enough to cover deductibles or Insurance shortfalls without special assessments.

Boards should not tell owners what limit to buy, because each owner's needs and policy differ. They can encourage owners to compare their HO-6 policy with the master policy every year and to consult an Insurance agent about aligning the two, as Colorado recommends.
